Auraworld  ·  Rescue World

We studied how living systems find what matters—and used that lesson to help AI remember the right decision at the right time.

The path led from growth intelligence, to tests of AI teamwork, to a seventy-two-hour earthquake simulation, and finally to a simple action card that worked in every tested handoff.

01  ·  What living systems taught us

Send attention toward pressure, uncertainty and new information.

Roots, fungi and slime moulds sense local conditions and extend themselves toward useful signals. We translated that behavior into AI teamwork: keep several possibilities alive, revisit the uncertain parts, and direct review toward the evidence that can change the next action.

Our earlier tests turned that idea into three practical rules. Keep evidence attached to each idea. Keep unanswered questions visible. Let the AI repair one failed step without restarting the whole task.

02  ·  From a benchmark to an emergency

We gave the AI a current picture it could use to make the next decision.

An AI team is several AI roles sharing one task: some read reports, some check the findings, and one chooses an action. Every finding carried its source, place, quantity and unanswered questions. Ordinary software checked whether the proposed action followed the exercise's evidence, resource and timing rules.

This created a clear chain from a public report, to an AI interpretation, to a proposed action, to a checked change inside the simulation. It also let us test whether that chain could survive across many connected decisions.

The next test kept the same emergency alive from the first decision through the seventy-two-hour close.

03  ·  The continuous disaster test

The same AI team stayed with one earthquake for all seventy-two hours.

Rescue World replays 414 public-record events from the first three days after the 2026 Kumamoto earthquake. Inside that fixed timeline, AI teams faced eleven decisions about fire crews, missing readings, collapsed buildings, shelter pressure, aftershocks and water delivery.

Each team saw the information available at that moment. A proposal changed the simulation only after it passed the evidence, resource and timing checks. Earlier assignments then shaped which actions were still possible later.

We completed 32 full runs of the seventy-two-hour emergency, containing 352 checked decision moments. Every new decision received the reports available then, the assignments already made and the questions still unanswered.

The clock was compressed, so the seventy-two-hour replay completed at model speed.

04  ·  The operational challenge

Every new decision depended on work that had already happened.

Fire and collapse response gave way to missing sensor reports, shelter pressure, water shortages, an aftershock and a final change in mission. A crew assigned early remained unavailable elsewhere. An unanswered question stayed open. A proposal that failed the rules changed nothing.

The test showed why follow-through matters. The AI handling a later moment needed to know exactly what had already been promised, why it had been promised and what it now had to do.

All reported effects are modeled task units inside the exercise.

05  ·  The handoff that exposed the gap

The system failed to act on an earlier crew assignment when confirmation became due.

At 20:00, the AI had to place response groups across two collapsed buildings. The paired decision then asked it to confirm a group already assigned to the paper mill.

The paper-mill confirmation stayed unresolved in all 32 complete runs. We then isolated eight saved histories in which the earlier paper-mill assignment was valid. Those eight histories gave us a fair test of whether the later AI could use that exact assignment.

06  ·  The action card

We turned the earlier assignment into a clear piece of work that had to be completed.

Most AI teamwork systems pass notes or summaries forward. During a long task, the next AI usually receives a large history and must work out which earlier detail matters now.

Our system creates an action card whenever a decision creates future work. The card says what was decided, which resource and place it concerns, which reports support it, what remains unknown and what must happen next.

When that next step becomes possible, the card goes directly to the AI making the decision. The AI must confirm or decline it, and software checks the answer.

The important decision was no longer background history. It arrived as work that had to be completed.

Qwen3.5-122B also completed the exact confirmation in all eight histories on its first attempt. Seven of eight safety replies passed every rule. Neither model falsely claimed that an unresolved task was complete.

08  ·  Turning the experiment into a tool

Rescue World makes the result something a person can inspect.

The plain-English story comes first: what was happening, what the AI proposed, how much it proposed, where it would go, whether the simulation accepted it, why, and what remained unanswered.

The 3D environment shows the disaster situation. The combined graph shows the agent teamwork behind a proposal and the modeled demands connected to it. These visual aids support the plain-English explanation.

Model–Move–Mesh gives a reader three simple questions: Model—what did the AI believe from the reports available then? Move—what exact proposal did it make? Mesh—did the supporting reports, unanswered questions and earlier assignments reach this decision intact?

The human-readable disaster story leads every view. The 3D world and network graph provide context for inspection.

09  ·  The finding and the product

Important decisions should automatically return when it is time to act on them.

When an AI makes a decision that will matter later, the system should create a clear action card and deliver it to the person or AI handling the next step exactly when they need it.

Rescue World lets a person follow the emergency, inspect the AI proposal, read the action card, see whether the simulation accepted the answer and understand what remains open.

Evidence boundary

We demonstrated the action card on one exact handoff, eight saved versions of the situation and two Qwen models from the same model family. The findings describe decisions inside this exercise; they do not yet establish performance in other emergencies.

Next research step: repeat the mechanism across a second incident and additional model families.
