Auraworld

This is how the project grew, in five steps.

We started with a question about how living networks grow. We finished with a tool that shows how decisions were made during a real earthquake. Each step below grew out of the one before it.

Scroll down, or press the down arrow key.

Step one of five  ·  The idea

We asked whether fleets of AI agents work better when they copy living networks.

Slime moulds, fungal networks and tree roots find good routes through changing ground using simple local rules, with no manager telling any part of the network what to do.

We wrote three experiments to test whether a fleet of AI agents would work better under the same rules, and we wrote down what we expected before any agent ran.

Most of those written predictions turned out to be wrong, because capable agents already repeated the evidence inside their own writing and covered the gap the growth rules were meant to close.

That honest miss pushed the work toward a sharper question: what has to travel between two agents so that a guess does not quietly turn into a fact?

Step two of five  ·  The real gains

Checking the handoff between agents produced the first real gain.

We built teams of AI agents that had to name the cause of a computer outage, choose the repair, and name the document that proved it.

The agents sent each other short cards marking every possible cause as supported, rejected, or still undecided. Ordinary code then checked each card before the next agent was allowed to act.

We broke the links between an agent's answer and the evidence behind it, to see which teams noticed.

48.6 Score for teams that passed the broken handoff straight along, out of a hundred points.
96.5 Score on the same broken cases when ordinary code rebuilt and checked the links first.

On the cases where nothing was broken, the checking cost almost nothing, so the whole gain came from catching a failure that would otherwise have travelled onward.

Step three of five  ·  The real disaster

We took that finding to a real earthquake in southern Japan.

On 28 July 2026 an earthquake of magnitude 7.1 struck Kumamoto Prefecture. In the hours that followed, fire agencies, defence units and town halls made a long chain of choices while the information in front of them was still incomplete.

We rebuilt the first three days from public records, then sealed the viewer record as 414 numbered events: 403 public-record events and 11 later simulated decision points.

That file is sealed with a fingerprint of its own contents, so any later edit shows up and nobody can quietly rewrite what happened.

One fact in the record set the direction of everything after it: most of the deaths were in towns that the first official readings did not single out.

Step four of five  ·  The simulated decisions

AI agents then answered the same decisions the real responders faced.

We took five moments from the earthquake record where a real person had to commit to a choice, and gave the agents only the public information that existed before each reconstructed deadline. Two scored moments also used explicit exercise assumptions where the public record did not reveal what a real desk knew.

Every answer was graded against rules we wrote down before the run, and both of us checked the grading independently afterwards.

0 of 40 Answers that passed every rule when the agents worked from plain written notes.
17 of 40 Answers that passed when they worked from a table of checked evidence instead.
34 of 40 Answers that passed after one short correction message pointed out the broken rule.

The table of checked evidence also lost ground on one rule about staying inside the allowed limits, so we recorded that claim as a failure.

Step five of five  ·  The interface

The last step was making all of it readable by a person.

The whole run plays back in one window, moment by moment, at a pace a person can follow.

When a decision arrives, a card names who was deciding, what they knew at that minute, what they chose, and what else was open to them.

Nothing from later in the timeline is allowed onto that card, so you only ever see what the decider could see.

Two readers with no knowledge of this project judged the screen text one sentence at a time. The second reading marked 1,191 sentences clear, 34 vague and 3 unreadable; all 37 remaining problems were then repaired. The finished mechanical audit now lists 1,227 visible strings and finds zero failures, while quotations from the recording remain unchanged.

Where this arrived

This is now a tool for reading decisions made under pressure.

It holds a real disaster still, replays the moments where somebody had to commit, and shows how a fleet of AI agents answered using only the public information available by each reconstructed deadline; two scored moments also used explicit exercise assumptions. It is worth having when a wrong choice is expensive and somebody will later ask what the choice was based on.

One honest boundary

The grading measures whether a decision can be traced back to what was known at the time, and whether it followed the rules written before the run. It does not grade the real responders, and it is no evidence that an AI system would have saved lives in Kumamoto.
