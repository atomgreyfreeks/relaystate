01What this isFinishing Rescue World before we submit

We rebuilt a real earthquake response, and software agents worked through eleven moments of decision.

On 28 July 2026 a magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck Kumamoto Prefecture in southern Japan. Rescue World replays 414 recorded moments from the seventy-two hours that followed, over the real ground where they happened.

Eleven of those moments are points where somebody had to decide something. At each one, software agents answered the same question the real responders faced, holding only the information that was public by that moment's deadline.

Rules written before the runs graded every answer by machine. The choice the real responders actually made is always shown first, and no screen ranks an agent against a person.

One earthquake is replayed over the real ground of Kumamoto. A fleet of artificial-intelligence agents reads the reports, decides, and sends crews out. The evidence table those agents keep is grown the way a lichen grows. Claims sit on one side and sources on the other, so that neither one can spoil the other.

Measured

What a judge should leave believing

02The frameModel, move, mesh

Three plain questions hold everything we built and everything we measured.

Randy asked an artificial-intelligence system that knows nothing about this build to think about disaster-response behavior from scratch. Its write-up is outside thinking, and we credit it as such. It arrived at three questions, and at three foundations we had already built.

Model

Am I representing reality correctly?

This is where a mistake gets caught and corrected.

Move

Am I turning what I believe into the right amount of action, at the right time?

This is where a deadline decides what counts as fair.

Mesh

Am I making everyone working on this behave as one group?

This is where a handoff either carries its sources or loses them.

Already built

The outside write-up asked for three things this build already does.

  • It asks for two timelines, so that what was really happening stays separate from what each person believed at that moment. Our deadline rule does this: a decision is graded only on the information public at its own deadline.
  • It asks for decision episodes that explain why a timeline went a different way. Our trace does this in six steps: the real recorded choice first, what was known, what each way of working proposed, what the check caught, the correction, and the final action.
  • It asks that a stand-in decision-maker be given the same imperfect information the real one had. Our recorded answers do this: the agents answered each moment eight separate times, holding only the reports that were public when that moment's deadline passed.

Sources: the outside document Randy shared, read line by line against this build; board messages 1564, 1565 and 1589.

03What we measuredTwo behaviors and one lens

Two small habits changed how many answers passed every prewritten check.

Forty means five decision moments, each answered eight separate times, by one model working three different ways. Code did the grading, by rules written down before any run started.

Measured

Habit one: bind every claim to its source before you hand it on.

Every claim travels with the source it came from and with the things still unknown. This answers the mesh question: a group stays coherent only when each handoff carries its own sources.

0of 40 → 17of 40

The figures count answers that passed every prewritten check, out of forty tries. The one change was the desk keeping that evidence table.

  • Answers that broke at least one rule about sources, unknowns or allowed choices fell from 40 of 40 to 19 of 40.
  • Of the unknowns each moment required an answer to name out loud, plain notes named none. The evidence table named 97 in every 100.

Source: the frozen results at experiments/kumamoto-real-response/PRODUCTION-RESULTS.md, in the table of registered outcomes.

Measured

Habit two: name the mistake, fix it once, then check again.

One exact mistake goes back to the desk, one bounded correction comes forward, and the answer is checked again. This answers the model question, because it is how a wrong reading of reality gets repaired.

17of 40 → 34of 40

The figures count the same thing, after one mechanical message named the exact rule that was broken.

  • It repaired 17 of the 23 answers that came back broken, and it broke none of the answers that already worked.
  • It used at most one extra message per answer, and the length of the work grew 1.435 times against a ceiling of 1.5 set before the runs.

Source: the frozen results at experiments/kumamoto-real-response/PRODUCTION-RESULTS.md, in the table for the one-correction claim.

The deadline rule covered both habits: across all forty registered tries, the checker found zero answers that used a report which arrived later.

04What we did not measureStated before anyone asks

Here is exactly what those numbers do not say.

These checks measure traceability and rule-following inside the exercise. They do not grade the real responders or prove that an agent's judgment was better.

That sentence is pinned under every grade the build shows, word for word. It comes from docs/rescueworld/STORY-TEMPLATE.md and two automatic checks assert it.

Every number counts what software wrote down.

Each of the three figures you just saw counts answers written by software agents. No figure on any of these sections counts a change in how a person behaves. A viewer practises judging a choice by its deadline while watching, and we have never tested whether a person carries that habit out of the room.

One of the two registered claims failed its own rule.

Answers that stayed inside the exercise's hard resource limits fell from 37 of 40 with plain notes to 33 of 40 with the evidence table. The rule written before the runs allowed a drop of five points and the drop was ten, so that claim fails. Most of the failure sits in one decision about sending extra water trucks. We report it at the same size as the claim that passed.

The recording holds no count of people reached.

Nothing in this build claims that anyone was helped, or that one way of working would have helped more people than another. Whatever a decision changes, it changes inside the simulation only, and the screen says so wherever that shows.

The exercise is narrow on purpose.

One open model answered five decision moments rebuilt from the public record, eight separate times each. Two of those moments carry assumptions written for the exercise, because the public record does not show what the real desks held. This is one small sealed test, and a whole disaster holds far more decisions than five.

Sources: the frozen results at experiments/kumamoto-real-response/PRODUCTION-RESULTS.md, both in the registered outcomes and in the limits written at the end; docs/rescueworld/SPACE-DATA-STORIES.md, story four.

06The next validationExplain the finished run before coaching a live one

Next we test whether the method can explain why a finished decision mattered.

Proposed. Nothing here has been built or measured yet.

The current build can prove that a software answer followed its sources and rules. It cannot yet prove that the answer would have improved the disaster response. The safest next experiment is therefore a post-run debrief: let the run finish, then explain one decision using only facts the record and a controlled replay can verify.

  • Evidence: what was actually known by the deadline.
  • Comparison: the prewritten rule or normal plan the choice is compared with.
  • Gap: the specific mismatch the check found.
  • Feasible option: one alternative the available crews, roads and time really allowed.
  • Window: when that alternative could still have been chosen.
  • Tested effect: what changed when only that choice was altered in a controlled replay.

We would first run this after the event, where no live operator can be steered by an explanation that later turns out to be wrong. Only after the debrief proves high precision, no knowledge from the future, and trustworthy controlled replays would we test a live decision card.

The finished product supplies the starting evidence, not the final causal claim.

Four pieces already exist and make the next test possible.

  • The set of reports each moment held, cut off at that moment's deadline.
  • The real recorded choice shown first, beside twenty-four already graded software answers.
  • The checkers, which grade an answer in code by rules fixed in advance.

What still has to be built is the controlled replay that changes one admissible choice while holding everything else fixed. Until that exists, the product may explain rule-following but may not claim that a different choice caused a better outcome.

Source: Disaster Simulation Clarification.pdf, recommendation to begin with verified post-run debriefs and move to live decision cards only after precision, hindsight and counterfactual validation.

The first success test is narrow and falsifiable.

For the same five registered decision moments, the debrief must name the right evidence, find the right gap, propose only an option the record allowed, use nothing learned after the deadline, and predict an effect that survives the controlled replay. We measure those five things separately. A beautiful explanation that misses any one of them does not pass.
