01What this isFinishing Rescue World before we submit

We rebuilt three days of a real earthquake response, then asked AI agents what help they would send.

On 28 July 2026 a magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck Kumamoto Prefecture in southern Japan. Rescue World replays 414 saved events from the seventy-two hours that followed, over the real ground where they happened.

The viewer reconstructs eleven urgent decision moments from public records. AI agents later proposed what help to send using information public by each deadline. Five decisions formed the planned test; two of those also used clearly labeled exercise assumptions. Six more decisions provide unscored context.

Fixed code graded every AI proposal against rules written before the test. The viewer always labels the public response and the later AI proposal separately. No AI proposal shown here moved a real crew.

The orchestration idea is simple: keep every claim beside the report it came from, preserve what remains unknown, and return one exact error when a proposal breaks a rule. Rescue World makes that process visible inside a concrete disaster-response story.

Measured

What a judge should leave believing

Two small changes to agent orchestration made proposals far more likely to follow their sources and rules. This build shows the gain, the failure beside it, and exactly what the test never measured.

02The frameModel, move, mesh

Three plain questions show what the build proves—and what it still cannot answer.

A useful disaster system must keep its picture of events honest, turn that picture into a feasible action, and carry the evidence cleanly between agents.

Model

Am I representing reality correctly?

This is where a mistake gets caught and corrected.

Move

Am I turning what I believe into the right amount of action, at the right time?

This is where a deadline decides what counts as fair.

Mesh

Am I making everyone working on this behave as one group?

This is where a handoff either carries its sources or loses them.

What exists now

Rescue World tests a narrow part of each question.

  • Model: each decision receives only information public by its deadline, plus any exercise assumption shown on screen.
  • Move: each AI rescue proposal is checked against fixed limits on units, destinations, deadlines, sources and unknowns.
  • Mesh: an evidence table keeps every claim beside its source when work passes between steps.
  • Not built yet: the viewer does not reconstruct anyone's private beliefs or simulate what would happen after an AI proposal.

Sources: docs/rescueworld/ACTION-FIRST-PRESENTATION-CONTRACT.md and docs/rescueworld/STORY-TEMPLATE.md.

03What we measuredTwo behaviors and one lens

Two small habits changed how many answers passed every prewritten check.

Forty means five decision moments, each answered eight separate times, by one model working three different ways. Code did the grading, by rules written down before any run started.

Registered claim: failed — four of five tests passed

Habit one: bind every claim to its source before you hand it on.

Every claim travels with the source it came from and with the things still unknown. This answers the mesh question: a group stays coherent only when each handoff carries its own sources.

0of 40 → 17of 40

The figures count AI proposals that passed every rule, out of forty tries. The only change was keeping each claim beside its source and naming what remained unknown.

  • Answers that broke at least one rule about sources, unknowns or allowed choices fell from 40 of 40 to 19 of 40.
  • Of the unknowns each moment required an answer to name out loud, plain notes named none. The evidence table named 97 in every 100.

Source: experiment/PRODUCTION-RESULTS.md, planned-test results.

Registered claim: passed — five of five tests passed

Habit two: name the mistake, fix it once, then check again.

Fixed code returns one exact rule error. The same AI revises once using the same reports, then the code checks the proposal again.

17of 40 → 34of 40

The figures count the same thing, after one mechanical message named the exact rule that was broken.

  • It repaired 17 of the 23 answers that came back broken, and it broke none of the answers that already worked.
  • It used at most one extra message per answer, and the length of the work grew 1.435 times against a ceiling of 1.5 set before the runs.

Source: experiment/PRODUCTION-RESULTS.md, one-error-message result.

Across all three approaches, the checker found no scored proposal that used a report published after its deadline.

04What we did not measureStated before anyone asks

Here is exactly what those numbers do not say.

These checks measure traceability and rule-following inside the exercise. They do not grade the real responders or prove that an agent's judgment was better.

That sentence is pinned under every grade the build shows, word for word. It comes from docs/rescueworld/STORY-TEMPLATE.md and two automatic checks assert it.

Every number counts what software wrote down.

Each of the three figures you just saw counts answers written by software agents. No figure on any of these sections counts a change in how a person behaves. A viewer practises judging a choice by its deadline while watching, and we have never tested whether a person carries that habit out of the room.

One prediction written before the runs failed.

Answers that stayed inside the exercise's hard resource limits fell from 37 of 40 with plain notes to 33 of 40 with the evidence table. The rule written before the runs allowed a drop of five percentage points and the drop was ten, so that claim fails. Most of the failure sits in one decision about sending extra water trucks.

The experiment did not simulate who an AI proposal would reach.

The public record includes outcomes reported by real responders. The experiment did not simulate the later effects of any AI proposal, so it cannot say that one proposal would have helped more people than another.

The exercise is narrow on purpose.

One open-weight language model answered five decisions eight times each. Two decisions also used explicit exercise assumptions because public records did not reveal what the real offices knew. This is one small test, and a real disaster contains far more decisions.

Sources: experiment/PRODUCTION-RESULTS.md and docs/rescueworld/SPACE-DATA-STORIES.md, story four.

06The next validationExplain the finished run before coaching a live one

Next we test whether the method can explain why a finished decision mattered.

Proposed. Nothing here has been built or measured yet.

The current build measures whether an AI proposal followed its sources and rules. The next test needs a separate, expert-validated replay to measure what an allowed proposal changes.

  • Evidence: public information by the deadline, plus any labeled exercise assumption.
  • Comparison: the written rule or normal plan.
  • Gap: the exact mismatch.
  • Feasible option: an allowed alternative.
  • Window: when it could still be chosen.
  • Tested effect: what changed when only that choice changed.

We test this first as a post-run debrief. A live decision card waits until those explanations prove accurate and free of future information.

The current product supplies the starting evidence, not the outcome.

Three pieces already exist:

  • Reports cut off at each deadline.
  • The public-response record beside graded AI proposals.
  • Code that checks rules fixed before the runs.

Still missing: a controlled replay that changes one allowed choice, holds everything else fixed and produces an expert-validated outcome.

Sources: docs/rescueworld/STORY-TEMPLATE.md and docs/rescueworld/ACTION-FIRST-PRESENTATION-CONTRACT.md.

The first success test is narrow and falsifiable.

For the same five scored decisions, an explanation must use the right evidence, find the right gap, propose an allowed option, avoid future information and predict an effect the controlled replay reproduces.
